Case study

Teaching a computer to see a hophornbeam

Notes from building an offline visual identification trainer for 121 woody ornamentals — and the licensing dead-end that turned into the better idea.

Stack — single-file HTML, React (no build step), SVG Scope — 121 species, 484 generated plates Size — 251 KB, zero external requests

This started as a straightforward request: turn a 1988 college plant-ID course into something I could drill with again. It ended up being less about plants and more about what happens when you can't get the assets you planned on, and have to build the thing that makes them unnecessary.

The brief

HRT 116, Woody Ornamental Plants, was a two-credit summer course — a compressed few weeks covering roughly a hundred species, taught walk-and-name on live material with a practical identification final. Not a lecture course. That format mattered later: the trainer needed to reward identifying a plant on the least evidence possible, the way a field final does, not on recognizing a full glossy photograph. No partial credit for a passing glance, and none at all for leafing through a photo gallery until something looked familiar.

Where the photo plan broke

The first version leaned on real photographs — a manifest with 121 empty src slots, ready to fill from Wikimedia Commons. It didn't survive contact with the actual licensing landscape. A quick survey of Commons results for these species turned up mostly CC BY-SA 3.0, where share-alike would have obligated the whole project to redistribute under a compatible license. Getting around that meant hunting public-domain-only files one species at a time — dozens of searches, with the identification itself unverified at the end of each one. For a tool whose entire point is teaching correct IDs, shipping a guess felt worse than shipping nothing.

Decision

Stop trying to source 121 photographs. Solve the actual problem — resolving confusable species pairs — with something that doesn't depend on finding the right image at all.

Comparison mode

The photos were meant to help with one specific failure: two similar species getting mixed up. So that became the feature directly. Miss an identification and a "Compare side by side" button appears, putting the specimen you called next to the one it actually was — all four generated plates each, plus a diff table of only the characters that separate them.

Because it's derived from the morphological specs rather than authored per pair, it works for any of the 121 species against any other, with no hand-written comparison content to maintain.

The bug the comparison mode caught

Building comparison mode surfaced a real data error: Quercus rubra and Q. palustris were rendering almost identically. The lobe generator's exponent was inverted, giving the pointed-lobed species a shallower sinus instead of a narrower one. Once fixed, sinus depth became an actual parameter — white oak rounded and shallow, red oak cut about halfway, pin oak cut nearly to the midrib with narrow struts — and "lobing" is now a row in the diff table. All 484 plates were regenerated from the corrected spec.

The thirty photos that still matter

Line art can't do everything. Bark color and exfoliation pattern — paperbark maple's cinnamon curl, river birch's salmon-buff peel — are color characters a cyanotype line drawing deliberately can't carry. So the photo plan didn't disappear; it got smaller and more targeted. The sourcing worksheet narrows 121 species down to 30 images across three tiers: twelve confusable clusters where a photo resolves what the drawing can't, six bark shots where color is the identification, and an optional seasonal set.

Two small scripts enforce the discipline around that: a license auditor that classifies every manifest URL as clean, attribution-required, share-alike, or blocked, and fails the build on any of the last two — and a build script that inlines whatever's been sourced and verified into the trainer as a sidecar file. As of this writing the manifest's src fields are still blank on purpose: confirming that a photograph really is Ostrya and not Carpinus is the one step that can't be automated, and it's the step where an error would teach the wrong thing.

What the 1988 curriculum got right — and wrong

Cross-checking the species list against what a 1988 mid-Atlantic ornamental course would actually have taught turned up something the syllabus alone couldn't: five species on the list — Japanese barberry, burning bush, Callery pear, nandina, and Japanese privet among them — were standard nursery stock at the time and are now Maryland-regulated or regionally discouraged invasives. Eleven species across the full list carry that flag today, cross-referenced against the Maryland Department of Agriculture's current invasive plant list. The trainer keeps the course's original 121-species scope intact — it's still testing the curriculum as taught — but a specimen's verdict card now notes when its status has changed since.

What shipped

  • 121 species, 484 procedurally generated cyanotype plates (four views each)
  • Evidence-economy scoring — points reward identifying on less information, closer to how the original course actually tested
  • Comparison mode, derived from specs rather than authored per species pair
  • A 30-image photo worksheet with per-source licensing rules, plus a license auditor that blocks the build on a bad one
  • Eleven current invasive-status flags layered onto the original 1988 list
  • Single HTML file, 251 KB, no external requests, sessionStorage only

Built with Claude. The full build tooling — the sourcing worksheet, the license verifier, and the photo build script — ships alongside the trainer rather than staying private, on the theory that the process is worth as much as the artifact.
